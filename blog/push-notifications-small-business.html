Push Notifications That Customers Actually Welcome

By RMG LabsNovember 24, 20264 min read

Push notifications are the most powerful and the most easily abused feature in a small business app. Used well, a short message on a customer's lock screen brings them back on a slow Tuesday or tells them their order is ready. Used badly, it is the reason someone mutes your app or deletes it. This article covers when a notification earns its place, how often to send, and how to ask for permission without scaring people off.

Start With Messages People Want

The best notifications are ones the customer would have asked for if you had offered. Think of them in two groups. Transactional messages respond to something the customer did: “Your order is ready for pickup,” “Your appointment is tomorrow at 3:00,” or “You are one stamp away from a free drink.” Promotional messages come from you: a new menu item, a quiet-day offer, a seasonal event. Transactional messages are almost always welcome. Promotional ones have to earn it, so keep them rare and relevant.

Ask for Permission at the Right Moment

Notifications are opt-in. On iPhone the app must request authorization before it can alert anyone, and on Android 13 and later apps must ask for the notification permission at runtime (see Google’s notification permission documentation). That single system prompt is often your only chance, because a customer who taps “Don’t allow” has to dig through settings to change their mind.

So do not fire the prompt the moment the app opens. Wait until the value is obvious. If your app takes online orders, ask right after the first order is placed, with a line such as “Want a heads-up when this is ready?” shown before the system prompt. The customer understands the benefit, and your opt-in rate is likely to be better than a cold request on launch.

How Often Is Too Often?

There is no magic number, but a useful habit is to treat every promotional message as a withdrawal from a trust account. A small shop rarely has news worth more than a couple of promotional messages a week, and many have news worth far fewer. Watch for the warning signs: customers turning notifications off, uninstalls after a send, or replies asking you to stop. Those are the metrics that matter more than raw send counts.

Make Each Message Count

A good notification is short, specific and gives a reason to act now. Compare “Check out our deals!” with “Fresh cinnamon rolls out of the oven until 10 a.m.” The second one has a time limit, a real item and a clear action. Other habits that help:

  • Respect quiet hours. Schedule messages for sensible times in your customers’ time zone, not yours.
  • Link to the right screen. Tapping a loyalty reminder should open the loyalty card, not the home page.
  • Let people choose. A settings screen with separate switches for order updates, offers and reminders keeps customers who dislike one type from turning off all three.
  • Send to the right people. A message about a Saturday event matters most to customers near your shop who have visited before.

What It Takes to Build

Behind the scenes, notifications are delivered through the platform services run by Apple and Google, and most apps connect to them through a messaging service or a backend of their own. Costs and capabilities vary by provider and change over time, so check current pricing before you commit. The more important design question is what triggers a message: an order status changing, a booking reminder going out, a stamp being added to a loyalty card. Those triggers are where an app built around your business beats a generic broadcast tool. If you are still deciding what to build first, our guide to choosing between loyalty, ordering and booking is a good place to start.

A Real Example in Progress

These are the principles we keep in mind while building The Coffee Shop: order-ready alerts and loyalty reminders are the kind of useful, low-volume messages that suit a small business. The principle holds for any app: send less, make it relevant, and let customers stay in control.
